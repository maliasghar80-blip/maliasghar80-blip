Structure

The only 4 tabs you need

At seed stage a simple, clear model beats a big one. Each tab feeds the next, and every assumption sits in one place.

1 · Inputs
Every assumption: prices, conversion rates, churn, salaries, hiring dates. Blue font. Nothing is calculated here.
↓
2 · Revenue engine
Traffic → leads → customers → revenue, month by month. Customers lost to churn are subtracted here.
↓
3 · Team & costs
Hiring plan by role and start month, cost of goods sold (servers, payment fees, support), marketing and overheads.
↓
4 · Cash & summary
Monthly P&L, cash balance, burn, runway, and 4 to 6 charts for the deck.

Three formatting rules investors notice

Blue = inputs you typed
Black = formulas
Green = links to other tabs

Monthly columns for the first 24 months, then yearly for years 3 to 5. Nobody believes month 47.

03
Revenue

Build revenue from the bottom up

Top-down says "the market is huge, so we get a slice." Bottom-up says "this is how each customer arrives." Only bottom-up works in a model.

Top-down

"SME accounting in our region is a $2B market. If we capture 1%, that's $20M."

Problem: says nothing about how. Every competitor can write the same sentence.

Bottom-up

"$3,000 a month on ads brings 2,000 visitors. 4% start a trial and 20% of those pay $49 a month."

Strength: each number can be tested, and improving one improves the result.

The driver chain

New customers = Visitors × Visit→Trial % × Trial→Paid %
Customers(t) = Customers(t−1) + New − Customers(t−1) × Monthly churn %
MRR(t) = Customers(t) × ARPA

Use the same chain for any model. A marketplace uses listings → buyers → orders × take rate. A service business uses leads → calls → closed deals × average contract.

Where do the rates come from? In order of credibility: your own data from the last 3 to 6 months, a pilot or waitlist, a comparable company's public numbers, and only then industry benchmarks. Note the source of every rate in your assumption log (page 13).

Grow the inputs, not the output. For example, ad spend rises as you raise, and trial conversion improves by 0.25 points per quarter after the onboarding redesign. Every change has a reason you can say out loud.

Watch for this: when you scale ad spend 5x, cost per click rarely stays flat. Add a line that raises CPC by 10 to 20% at each spend step. It's the first thing a sharp investor asks.

04
Unit economics

CAC, LTV and payback: the numbers that hold up

CAC (paid) = Paid spend ÷ Customers from paid = 3,000 ÷ 16 = $187.50 LTV = ARPA × Gross margin ÷ Monthly churn = 49 × 80% ÷ 4% = $980 LTV : CAC = 980 ÷ 187.50 = 5.2x CAC payback = CAC ÷ (ARPA × Gross margin) = 187.50 ÷ 39.20 = 4.8 months

Paid customers are the 2,000 paid visitors × 4% × 20% = 16. Blended CAC, dividing spend by all 24 customers, gives $125 and makes you look better than you are. Investors know this, so show paid CAC.

Rule-of-thumb ranges investors use

MetricHealthyConcerning
LTV : CAC3x or moreBelow 1.5x
CAC payback (SMB SaaS)Under 12 monthsOver 24 months
Gross margin (software)70–85%Under 50%
Burn multiple (net burn ÷ net new ARR)Under 1.5Over 3

These are common rules of thumb, not laws. Ranges differ by sector and stage, so check them against companies like yours. At pre-seed, investors care more that your logic is sound than that you hit these numbers.

Costs

Team and costs: where burn really comes from

At most early-stage startups, people are the biggest cost. Get the hiring plan right and most of the cost model follows.

RoleStart monthBase salary / yrFully loaded / mo
Founder × 21$36,000$6,900
Senior engineer2$42,000$4,025
Growth marketer4$30,000$2,875
Customer success7$18,000$1,725

Fully loaded = base × 1.15, plus or minus benefits, payroll taxes and equipment. The multiplier varies by country (roughly 1.1–1.4), so use your own.

Three cost buckets

Cost of goods sold

Hosting, payment fees, third-party APIs, support. Scales with customers.

Growth

Ad spend, tools and content. Drives your funnel (ch. 3).

Overheads

Rent, legal, accounting, software. Mostly fixed. Step up when you hire.

Link, don't type: payment fees = revenue × fee %. Hosting = customers × cost per customer. Tie each variable cost to its driver so your margins stay honest as you scale.

06
Cash

Cash, runway and how much to raise

Profit is an opinion and cash is a fact. The cash line tells you when the company dies, so it matters most.

Net burn = Cash out − Cash in (per month) Runway = Cash balance ÷ Net burn Raise = (Monthly net burn × months to next milestone) + 20–25% buffer
Example

Cash $600k, net burn $40k a month → 15 months of runway. A raise normally needs to buy 18–24 months, so plan to raise again with at least 6 months left.

Ask for a milestone, not a number

"$1.2M takes us to $100k MRR in 20 months, which is the metric next-round investors look for." That's an argument. "$1.2M" alone is not.

Use of funds (example $1.2M seed)

BucketShareAmountBuys

Scenarios

Scenarios that survive

One forecast is a guess. Three scenarios show you've thought about what goes wrong, and what you'd do about it.

InputDownsideBaseUpside
Visit → trial3.0%4.0%5.0%
Trial → paid15%20%24%
Monthly churn6%4%3%
Cost per click$2.00$1.50$1.30
HiringDelayed 3 moPlanPlan

Build it in 10 minutes

  • Put the three columns on the Inputs tab, with one cell saying 1, 2 or 3.
  • Use =CHOOSE(scenario, downside, base, upside) to feed the live input.
  • The whole model switches when you change one cell. Investors love this in a live meeting.

The downside rule: in your downside case, show what you'd cut (delay hires, reduce ad spend) to keep at least 9–12 months of runway. Showing you can survive the downside builds more trust than an impressive upside.

The meeting

Defending your numbers: 10 questions to prepare for

They askA strong answer points to
1. What has to be true for this revenue?The 3 drivers, and evidence for each
2. Where does your conversion rate come from?Your own data or a pilot, with dates
3. Why does CAC stay flat as you scale?"It doesn't. We step CPC up 15% per spend tier."
4. What if growth is half your plan?Your downside scenario and what you'd cut
5. When do you need to raise again?Runway month and the milestone you'll hit first
6. What's your biggest cost and why?The hiring plan, tied to product or revenue goals
7. How did you size the team?Workload per hire (e.g. 1 support hire per 400 customers)
8. Why this amount and not half?What half buys, and why that milestone is too weak
9. How does gross margin improve?Specific cost lines that fall per customer at scale
10. Which assumption are you least sure of?Name it honestly, and say how you'll test it

Question 10 is a trust test. "None, we're confident" is the wrong answer. Naming your weakest assumption, with a plan to test it, shows you understand your own business.

Tool 3

Before you hit send: 15 checks

  • Every input is on the Inputs tab, in blue
  • No numbers typed inside formulas
  • Revenue comes from a driver chain, not a % growth rate
  • Churn is subtracted every month
  • CPC or CAC rises as spend scales
  • Variable costs are linked to customers or revenue
  • Hiring plan has start months
  • Cash tab shows a monthly balance
  • Runway month is clearly labelled
  • Raise amount = burn × months + buffer
  • Use of funds adds up to 100%
  • Downside case keeps 9 or more months of runway
  • Years 3–5 are rounded and yearly
  • Assumption log is filled in
  • You've written answers to all 10 questions

Last step: send the model to one finance-minded friend and ask: "Which number do you believe least?" Fix that one before investors see it.

Tool 4

Glossary: plain-English definitions

MRR / ARR: monthly or annual recurring revenue. ARR = MRR × 12.

ARPA: average revenue per account, per month.

CAC: customer acquisition cost. Spend ÷ customers won.

LTV: lifetime value. Gross profit a customer brings before they leave.

Churn: % of customers (or revenue) lost each month.

Gross margin: (Revenue − COGS) ÷ Revenue.

COGS: costs that rise with each sale or customer.

Net burn: cash out minus cash in, per month.

Runway: months until cash hits zero at the current burn.

CAC payback: months of gross profit needed to earn back CAC.

Burn multiple: net burn ÷ net new ARR. Lower is better.

Default alive: you reach profit before cash runs out, without new funding.

Use of funds: how the raise is split across spending categories.

Bottom-up: revenue built from customer-level drivers.

Scenario: a full set of inputs (downside, base or upside) that drives the model.

Fully loaded cost: salary plus benefits, taxes and equipment.
